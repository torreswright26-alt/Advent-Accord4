# Lighting, Color, and Mood

## Lighting
Use lighting to establish mood before adding particles or atmospheric clutter.

Approved strategies:
- warm practical light in interiors,
- broad window light,
- overcast diffuse light,
- cold moonlight,
- reflected rain light,
- dawn haze,
- hard midday sun,
- storm contrast,
- snow bounce,
- firelight,
- deep blue hour,
- mist with restrained diffusion.

## Color
- Colors may pop.
- Saturation should be selective.
- Maintain clear temperature structure.
- Preserve local material identity.
- Avoid universal brown-gray desaturation.
- Avoid defaulting to dark blue-black scenes.
- Do not allow clean hierarchy to become a narrow “safe” palette.
- Preserve deep colored shadow interiors instead of filling shadows with flat
  black, gray, or a single blue.
- Across a body of work, vary saturation, temperature, local color, time of
  day, weather, and emotional register deliberately.

## Emotional Range

The renderer must not imply happy adventure by default. It should support:

- beauty, romance, leisure, celebration, and tenderness;
- tension, uncertainty, fatigue, grief, danger, corruption, and awe;
- mature quiet without boredom;
- spectacle without universal glow or visual noise.

The requested scene controls mood. The rendering style must not force every
scene toward friendliness, gloom, golden-hour wonder, or heroic optimism.

## Mood Dataset Goal
Mood references should vary weather, time, and illumination while retaining the same render language.
